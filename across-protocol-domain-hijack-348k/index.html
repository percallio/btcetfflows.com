Victim Self-Check: Have I Signed on across.to?

Do this before anything else:

  1. Open your wallet's activity / signature history (Phantom, MetaMask, Rabby, or the block explorer for your chain).
  2. Filter for approvals and permit signatures that mention across.to or Across protocol contracts — especially EIP-2612 permit signatures, which move USDC/ERC-20 balance without a visible token transfer in the same transaction.
  3. Look for the window: signatures dated around the evening of 11-10 (local time ± a few hours) are the ones inside the drainer window.
  4. If you find a permit you don't recognize: screenshot it, note the token, amount, and spender address, then revoke the approval (your wallet's built-in revoke, or a revocation tool).
  5. Check balances, not just signatures. A drained permit means the tokens already moved; the revocation stops the next use.

This matters because of where the loss concentrated: 88% of the total — about $305K — came from a single Aave depositor's permit signature [Bitquery]. One blind permit, one wallet, nearly the whole incident.

How a Domain Hijack Becomes a Drainer

  1. Domain takeover. The attacker gained control of across.to (lapsed DNS, registrar takeover, or compromised credentials — the exact vector is the incident's operational detail) and pointed it at their own drainer front-end.
  2. The drainer looks official. Same URL, same branding. Users who had a tab open, or who revisited to check "is it back up?", loaded the drainer.
  3. Permit-based theft. Modern drainers prefer EIP-2612 permit flows: the user signs "approve this spender to move X of my USDC" — a permission, not a visible transfer. The drainer holds the permit, then sweeps the balance in a second, separate transaction.
  4. Cash-out in 80/20. The $348K was split 80/20 — the classic rented-drainer-kit split (80% to the exploit operator, 20% to the kit provider) — then moved to Monero coins, the Rainbet casino, and BTC [Bitquery].

The $294K number floating around

Some posts cite $294K. That figure is the old balance of the fee wallet that the drainer passed through — not additional victim funds [Bitquery]. The victim total is $348K.

Why This Page Stays Useful

Domain hijacks on dapp front-ends are a repeat pattern: the protocol is often fine, the website is the attack surface, and the signature is the weapon. Once you can read a permit signature and a revocation screen, you are covered against the next one — on any dapp, not just Across.

FAQ

Was the Across protocol contract hacked? No — the loss came from users' own permit signatures on the hijacked front-end [Bitquery].

How many wallets were hit? Nine wallets across five chains [Bitquery].

Where did the money go? 80/20 split, then Monero coins, Rainbet, and BTC [Bitquery].

Is $294K a second loss figure? No — it's the fee wallet's pre-existing balance, not victim funds [Bitquery].

I signed on across.to weeks ago — am I affected? An old permit to the legitimate contract is normal and expected. The risk window is signatures made during the hijack (evening of 11-10); older permits to the known-good address are fine, but worth a glance.
