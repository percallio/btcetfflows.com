Initia Cloud-Credential Breach: $3.27M via Forged Rollup Withdrawals

Written by Crypto Desk · Last verified: 2026-11-13 (disclosure cycle 11-09 → 11-12, data pulled 2026-11-12 17:49–18:10 UTC)

Answer (as of 2026-11-12 18:10 UTC):

On 9 November 2026 an attacker used a compromised Google Cloud Platform key (command injection) to reach Initia's infrastructure, then forged IBC withdrawals from five rollups, moving about $3.27M to attacker addresses on Initia L1 — of which about $2.16M remains frozen on the paused chain (the two figures are the same money at different stages, not two losses). It is the second exploit in under three weeks — the first, on 23 October, took ~$2.7M via a MoveVM script-cache bug — and the L1 has now been halted twice in a month (second halt 06:18 UTC on 11-10). INITIA traded at $0.1129 (CG rank 704), -23.2% over 24h, $24.8M market cap, $62.9M volume, -92.1% from its ATH. Initia says it will restore the backing of affected assets in full.

This page is the security-cluster record for the event: the timeline, the attack mechanics (the part that stays useful after the price noise), what the two dollar figures actually mean, a self-check for users, and the token data.

How the Attack Worked (mechanics — stays useful)

  1. Entry: a provision key. The compromised GCP key was used for provisioning private nodes. Command injection on it exposed environment variables — the classic cloud-misconfig cascade.
  2. Credential chaining. The exposed variables opened the team's GitHub and GCP accounts, which yielded further credentials — one leaked variable can be a key ring.
  3. Reach: Anvil. With those credentials the attacker reached Anvil, the hosting platform for Initia's rollups — the off-chain control plane, not the on-chain consensus.
  4. The forgery: IBC. From there, forged IBC withdrawals from five rollups were validated, releasing assets held in the L1's IBC escrow accounts — money bridged into rollups, held in escrow on the L1.
  5. Cash-out at the rate limit. The attacker sold part of the loot on Initia's own DEX, then bridged out 13,400 TIA, 8,000 USDC.inj and 597,000 Noble USDC — exactly at Initia's outbound IBC rate limits. No INIT was withdrawn.

The pattern matters: the chain's consensus was never broken. The attack surface was cloud infrastructure + a rollup host — the same family of failure as the 10-23 MoveVM cache bug, just a different layer. For anyone operating a modular stack, the lesson is that the escrow's value is only as good as the off-chain control plane in front of it.

What the Two Dollar Figures Mean

$3.27M = the total moved to the attacker's Initia L1 address. $2.16M = the part of that total still sitting on the paused L1, where it cannot be moved. The difference (~$1.1M) is what was already sold on the DEX or bridged out through the rate-limited IBC channels. All four sources (CoinDesk, BlockBeats, ForkLog, CMC) carry both numbers consistently — they are one amount at two stages, not two losses. Initia's stated remedy: "restore the backing of affected assets in full" (plan details still being finalized), infrastructure rebuilt from scratch in an isolated environment, least-privilege access enforced, onchain IBC validation hardened, law enforcement and security partners engaged. The team expects recovery to take longer than a regular restart; no timeline published yet.

User Self-Check (compact)

  1. Did you bridge into an Initia rollup, or hold funds in a rollup whose assets settle in Initia L1 escrow? If yes — your funds are inside the affected escrow; the five affected rollups are not named in the public disclosure (awaiting verification), so check the rollup list on your bridge's status page after the restart.
  2. Do you hold INIT directly on the L1? The attacker withdrew no INIT — but the L1 was paused twice in a month, so unstaking/withdraw windows moved with the halts.
  3. Keep screenshots of balances before the next restart, and watch the official channel (X) for the finalized replenishment plan before moving size.
